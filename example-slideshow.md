# Write First, Present Later

A five-minute demo talk for Ariadne's presentation mode. You are reading the speaker notes: the audience never sees this text. Press Cmd+P (or the presentation icon) to present, move with the arrow keys, and press Esc to end.

Good morning. I want to show you a calmer way to make slides: you write the talk first, and the slides take care of themselves.

## The old way

- Open a blank deck and stare at it
- Fiddle with text boxes, fonts, and alignment
- Nudge pixels until midnight

We have all been there. The tool makes you design each slide by hand, so an hour disappears before you have said a single interesting thing. The slides become the work, instead of the talk.

## The new way

- Write the story as plain text
- Titles become slides, automatically
- Everything else is your private script

Here is the whole trick. You write one continuous document, exactly like this one. Each heading starts a new slide. The paragraphs underneath are speaker notes, visible only to you on the teleprompter.

## One radical idea

> Separate what you say from what you show.

Read that line out loud, then pause. The audience sees eleven words on a giant screen. You hold the full explanation in your notes, right where you need it, at the moment you need it.

That separation is the entire philosophy. Sparse slides, rich script. Your audience reads along; you never run dry.

## Titles become slides

```
## Titles become slides

This paragraph is a speaker note.
Only you ever see it.

- Bullets land on the slide.
- So do quotes, code, and images.
```

This slide is showing you its own source code. A fenced code block is a visual: it renders on the slide, monospaced, even though it contains things that look like headings and slide breaks. The parser knows better.

- **Bold** and plain bullets both work
- Numbered lists, checklists, and quotes too

## Notes stay private

- The teleprompter shows your script under each slide
- The timer keeps your five minutes honest
- The filmstrip jumps anywhere in one click

Look at your screen, not the projector. The audience sees the current slide and nothing else: no notes, no timer, no filmstrip. That asymmetry is what makes presenting feel safe.
